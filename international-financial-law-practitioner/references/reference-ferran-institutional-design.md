# “Institutional Design: The Choices for National Systems” — Eilís Ferran
**Format**: PDF text extraction | **Sections**: 10 thematic sections | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
There is no universally correct regulator chart. Institutional design allocates mandates, information, expertise, accountability, and crisis powers under local political and market conditions. Compare designs by coordination and incentive effects, not labels.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Allocation models
- **Institutional supervision** assigns regulators by firm type; it supports specialization but fits poorly when groups and products cross categories.
- **Functional supervision** assigns by financial function/activity; it can reduce boundary arbitrage but fragments oversight of one entity.
- **Objectives-based supervision** assigns prudential, conduct, competition, or stability objectives; it sharpens missions but creates interfaces.
- **Integrated regulator** consolidates multiple sectors/functions; it can pool information and expertise but may become unwieldy, dilute priorities, or concentrate failure.
- **Twin peaks** commonly separates prudential supervision from conduct/consumer regulation; it manages conflicting objectives but requires strong coordination.

### 2. Central-bank participation
- Central banks bring macroeconomic, payment-system, market, and emergency-liquidity information. Separation can protect monetary-policy focus or supervisory independence but risks crisis information gaps.
- Allocate macroprudential authority, microprudential supervision, lender-of-last-resort input, payments oversight, and resolution roles explicitly.

### 3. Mandates and conflicts
- Define statutory objectives, hierarchy/trade-offs, perimeter, rulemaking, supervision, investigation, enforcement, data, and emergency powers.
- Prudential, conduct, competition, innovation, inclusion, and stability goals can conflict. Do not hide value choices in vague “public interest” language.
- Prevent regulatory gaps, duplication, forum shopping, and responsibility shifting at boundaries.

### 4. Independence and accountability
- Independence requires appointment/removal protection, funding, operational autonomy, expertise, and resistance to industry and political capture.
- Accountability requires transparent objectives, reasons, reporting, legislative and judicial scrutiny, procedural fairness, review, and performance evaluation.
- Independence without accountability risks technocratic drift; accountability without operational independence invites short-term interference.

### 5. Coordination
- Use statutory duties, memoranda, colleges/committees, shared data, joint inspections, lead-regulator rules, referral/escalation, and crisis protocols.
- Coordination cost is real: clarify who decides, who can compel information, how disagreement is resolved, and who speaks in crisis.

## Worked Example
A country is redesigning oversight after failures at a bank-owned investment platform. Compare an integrated regulator, twin peaks, and institutional model against the actual failure channels: group capital/liquidity, product conduct, platform data, conflicts, operational resilience, competition, and crisis liquidity. Score information aggregation, conflicting mandates, boundary risk, accountability, transition cost, and central-bank coordination. Recommend not just an organogram but interface rules, data rights, escalation, and failure ownership.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When proposing a model, name the failure it solves and the new interface it creates.
- When mandates overlap, specify lead authority, information rights, escalation, and final decision.
- When invoking independence, pair it with reasons, transparency, review, and performance accountability.
- Verify current institutional allocations; restructurings can quickly date the source.

## Key Takeaways
1. Institutional architecture trades coordination inside agencies against coordination between them.
2. Clear mandates and interfaces matter more than fashionable labels.
3. Central-bank involvement should follow information and crisis-function needs.
4. Independence and accountability are complements, not opposites.
