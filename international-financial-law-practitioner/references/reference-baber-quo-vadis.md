# International Financial Law: Quo Vadis? — Graeme Baber
**Format**: PDF text extraction of available Chapters 1, 2, and 5 | **Sections**: 3 chapters | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
Baber frames international financial law as a cross-border field in which soft standards, global agenda-setting, specialist standard setters, monitoring, and domestic implementation form an emerging architecture. This module is deliberately limited to the three available chapters and reflects a 2017-era account.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Nature and boundaries of international financial law
- Baber defines the field around rules governing transactions with a cross-border element and a central transfer of money, and rules regulating money-related matters across borders.
- Distinguish international financial law from domestic financial law, non-financial international law, conflicts, comparative law, and regional systems; boundary choices require explanation.
- Soft-law principles, standards, codes, and guidelines occupy a central place, particularly after the global financial crisis.

### 2. Global financial architecture
- The G20 sets and reviews broad regulatory agenda; the Financial Stability Board coordinates vulnerability assessment, policy development, implementation monitoring, and outreach.
- Specialist institutions and committees include the IMF, World Bank, BIS/BCBS and other committees, IOSCO, IAIS, IASB, auditing, deposit-insurance, and anti-money-laundering bodies.
- Architecture is a network, not a hierarchy. For each output trace sponsor, drafter, addressee, implementation body, monitor, and consequence of divergence.
- Monitoring frameworks and peer review matter because international standards depend on national administration and implementation.

### 3. Basel Committee
- The **Basel Core Principles for Effective Banking Supervision** state minimum supervisory standards supported by essential and additional criteria and compliance gradings.
- The Basel framework’s three pillars address minimum capital requirements, supervisory review, and market discipline; later reforms added and revised capital, liquidity, leverage, and systemic-bank measures.
- Baber contrasts legal certainty and flexibility: detailed quantitative rules can promote comparability but may fit diverse systems poorly; principle-based standards adapt but allow inconsistent implementation.

### 4. Implementation audit
- For any international standard record legal status, precision, version/date, domestic implementing measure, responsible regulator, scope, transitional arrangements, supervisory practice, disclosure, monitoring, and review.
- **Anti-pattern — architecture as law**: listing bodies or standards without tracing the binding domestic rule and current version.

## Worked Example
To assess a jurisdiction’s Basel alignment, begin with the current BCBS text, then locate domestic legislation/rules, regulator guidance, firm-specific discretion, effective dates, and scope. Compare implementation with assessment/peer-review findings and explain deviations. Do not cite Baber’s historical Basel dates or ratios as current law; use the chapter to structure the institutional and implementation inquiry.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When a standard is called international law, state whether it is treaty, custom, institutional rule, soft standard, or domestic implementation.
- When describing an institution, distinguish agenda-setting, standard-setting, monitoring, financing, and enforcement roles.
- When precision increases, test adaptability and model risk; when flexibility increases, test consistency and accountability.
- Verify all current institutional memberships, texts, and Basel implementation dates.

## Key Takeaways
1. International financial law is institutionally networked and heavily reliant on soft standards.
2. Monitoring and domestic implementation give the architecture practical force.
3. The available source covers only Chapters 1, 2, and 5 and is historical orientation.
