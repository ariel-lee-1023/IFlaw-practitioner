# International Financial Institutions and International Law — Daniel D. Bradlow and David B. Hunter (eds.)
**Format**: Markdown extraction | **Sections**: 11 chapters plus introduction/conclusion | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
An international financial institution is simultaneously a treaty-based organization, a financial actor, a governance system, and a source of operational norms. Do not ask only whether an IFI is “bound by international law.” Identify the institution, mandate, legal personality, source of obligation, decision-maker, affected stakeholder, forum, immunity, and accountability route.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Institutional identity and mandate
- Start with constituent instrument, membership, voting, organs, delegated powers, financing function, and relationship agreements. Distinguish IMF stabilization functions from World Bank Group development functions and among IBRD, IDA, IFC, and MIGA roles.
- Apply **speciality/attributed powers**: an IFI acts within express powers and powers implied as necessary to perform its functions. Mission evolution requires a defensible link to mandate, not merely policy desirability.
- Internal policies and procedures can govern operations even when their external legal status is contested.

### 2. Sources of law applicable to IFIs
- Test treaty obligations, customary international law, general principles, constituent instruments, host/relationship agreements, internal law, contracts, and domestic law separately.
- Ask whether the rule binds the organization directly, binds member states acting through it, informs interpretation of mandate, or operates through lender policy/conditionality.
- **Anti-pattern — source collapse**: treating policy, best practice, customary law, and treaty obligation as interchangeable.

### 3. Legal personality, standing, and national courts
- International personality concerns capacity to hold rights and duties; domestic personality and capacity depend on constituent/implementing instruments and forum law.
- Before national courts, analyze standing, service, applicable law, waiver, immunity, and execution. Capacity to sue does not imply general amenability to suit.
- Borrowing and lending operations may receive different immunity treatment from governmental or employment functions; verify the specific instrument and forum jurisprudence.

### 4. Immunity and functional necessity
- **Functional necessity** links immunity to institutional independence, but overly broad immunity creates an accountability deficit.
- Separate immunity from jurisdiction, interim measures, and execution. Determine express waiver and whether an alternative dispute/accountability mechanism exists.
- A strong accountability design does not assume courts are always available; it combines reasoned internal decisions, transparency, review, remedy, and learning.

### 5. Responsibility and accountability
- Responsibility asks whether a legal obligation was breached and what consequences follow. Accountability is broader: explanation, participation, review, correction, and institutional consequences may exist without adjudicated responsibility.
- Attribute conduct to the organization or member states; identify primary obligation, breach, injury, causation, defenses, and remedy.
- Internal-policy non-compliance may support inspection or compliance review even where damages litigation is barred.

### 6. Financing operations and conditionality
- IMF arrangements and policy-based lending use finance to influence macroeconomic and institutional policy. Analyze legal form, conditions, monitoring, tranche/disbursement triggers, waiver power, and termination/suspension.
- Conditionality can serve resource protection and program effectiveness but may generate mandate, legitimacy, distribution, ownership, and human-rights concerns.
- Test whether conditions are necessary, tailored, evidence-based, transparent, internally authorized, and realistically implementable.

### 7. Structural adjustment and resource dependency
- Structural adjustment evolved from stabilization toward broader policy reform and debt relief. It can blur financing terms with domestic regulatory governance.
- Review policy leverage through the lenses of consent under dependency, mission creep, discretion, social distribution, and cumulative conditions across institutions.

### 8. Participation and administrative procedure
- Legitimate policy-making needs access to information, notice, consultation, reasoned response, documented decision, and access to independent review.
- Participation is not a ceremonial meeting. Match who participates, when, on what information, with what ability to affect scope and alternatives.
- Codified administrative procedures can improve predictability, fairness, institutional learning, and development effectiveness.

### 9. Human rights
- Separate direct organizational obligation, member-state treaty obligations, Charter-based arguments, policy commitments, and development-effectiveness rationales.
- Examine non-discrimination, participation, access to information, livelihood, labor, indigenous peoples, and economic/social rights throughout project and policy cycles.
- Avoid categorical claims that IFIs either bear all state-like duties or none; state the legal pathway and degree of confidence.

### 10. Indigenous peoples and FPIC
- Evaluate institutional safeguards against **free, prior and informed consent (FPIC)** standards and applicable law. Distinguish consultation from consent.
- Check early identification, culturally appropriate process, representative institutions, disclosure, benefit sharing, alternatives, grievance, and remedy.

### 11. Labor and environmental obligations
- Worker-rights analysis connects loan conditions, procurement, borrower practice, supervision, and complaint mechanisms.
- Environmental analysis links international norms, institutional operational policies, impact assessment, alternatives, mitigation hierarchy, supervision, and compliance review.
- A safeguard is only operational if the legal/financing documents, appraisal, covenants, monitoring budget, and remedy process reinforce one another.

## Worked Example
For a contested World Bank-financed infrastructure project, prepare an authority-and-accountability map. Identify the Bank entity and financing instrument; borrower and implementing agencies; applicable constituent and operational policies; environmental, labor, indigenous, and disclosure standards; member-state obligations; financing covenants; consultation record; supervision findings; and available inspection/compliance mechanisms. For each alleged harm, distinguish legal responsibility, internal-policy compliance, and broader accountability. Address immunity before proposing court litigation, and connect any remedy to the institution or borrower able to implement it.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When asserting an IFI obligation, name the source, addressee, binding pathway, and review forum.
- When mandate is disputed, connect the action to express or necessarily implied functions and identify limiting language.
- When immunity blocks adjudication, test waiver and alternative mechanisms; do not equate immunity with substantive lawfulness.
- When recommending conditionality, assess necessity, proportionality, ownership, distribution, monitorability, and cumulative burden.
- When safeguards matter, trace them into appraisal, contract, supervision, complaint, and remedy.

## Key Takeaways
1. “IFI law” is a layered system of constituent instruments, international law, internal rules, contracts, and domestic law.
2. Responsibility, immunity, and accountability are related but distinct questions.
3. Conditionality is both a financing-control mechanism and an exercise of governance power.
4. Participation and independent review are central sources of institutional legitimacy.
5. Human-rights and environmental claims require a precise legal pathway, not slogans.
