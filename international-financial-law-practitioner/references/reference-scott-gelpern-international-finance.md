# International Finance: Transactions, Policy, and Regulation — Hal S. Scott and Anna Gelpern
**Format**: Markdown extraction of 22nd edition | **Sections**: 20 chapters | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
International finance cannot be separated into “private deal” and “public regulation.” A transaction changes incentives and channels risk through institutions and infrastructure; regulation changes transaction design and may relocate rather than eliminate risk. Analyze every problem at three levels: firm/contract, market/infrastructure, and sovereign/international coordination.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Globalization and systemic risk
- Describe the transaction and market before applying doctrine: instruments, intermediaries, leverage, maturity/liquidity transformation, collateral, interconnectedness, and cross-border exposures.
- **Systemic risk** is the possibility that distress impairs critical financial functions and propagates through runs, fire sales, payment/settlement chains, correlated positions, or confidence effects.
- Use the crisis sequence: vulnerability accumulation → trigger → amplification → transmission → official response → distribution of losses → reform feedback.
- Distinguish microprudential protection of individual firms from macroprudential protection of system functions.

### 2. Cross-border regulatory perimeter
- For each activity identify home and host regulators, entity and activity classifications, territorial hooks, licensing, disclosure/conduct, prudential, resolution, and enforcement rules.
- National treatment, mutual recognition, substituted compliance/equivalence, harmonization, and extraterritoriality are alternative coordination technologies with different trust and sovereignty costs.
- Regulatory competition can discipline or weaken systems; test whether business migrates because of efficiency, legal clarity, subsidy, or underpriced externalities.

### 3. Banking markets and consolidated supervision
- Separate branch, subsidiary, representative-office, booking-location, and group exposures. Legal entity boundaries matter in insolvency even where risk is managed globally.
- Home supervisors typically oversee the consolidated group; hosts protect local entities, depositors, and stability. Information gaps and ring-fencing become acute in crisis.
- Analyze capital, liquidity, leverage, large exposures, governance, stress testing, and resolution as a connected stack rather than isolated ratios.

### 4. Financial-market infrastructure
- Payment, clearing, and settlement systems transform bilateral trades into timed obligations. Identify participant, operator, central counterparty, central securities depository, settlement bank, and central bank roles.
- Key risks: principal, replacement-cost, liquidity, custody, operational, legal, and systemic risk. Relevant controls include real-time gross settlement, netting, margin, default funds, delivery-versus-payment, payment-versus-payment, settlement finality, and recovery/resolution planning.
- New payment technologies change access and architecture but do not remove liquidity, fraud, operational, governance, or finality questions.

### 5. Capital adequacy, liquidity, and stress testing
- Risk-weighted capital absorbs loss but depends on definitions, risk weights, models, and incentives. Add leverage constraints because measured risk can be wrong; add liquidity rules because solvent firms can fail from cash-flow mismatch.
- Stress tests are scenario-dependent supervisory judgments, not forecasts. Inspect scenario severity, balance-sheet assumptions, model governance, management actions, disclosure, and consequences.
- **Anti-pattern — ratio formalism**: treating compliance with one metric as proof of resilience.

### 6. Euromarkets and syndicated lending
- Offshore markets may reduce regulatory frictions while creating supervisory and crisis-management gaps. Trace currency, booking office, borrower, lender, payment system, and applicable regulation separately.
- In syndicated credit, distinguish economic risk allocation from agent administration, voting, transfer, and enforcement rights. Review sample-document provisions against actual market and insolvency functions.

### 7. Securitization
- Map originator → special-purpose vehicle → securities → investors, alongside servicer, trustee, credit enhancement, liquidity support, ratings, and derivatives.
- Test legal isolation/true sale, bankruptcy remoteness, asset eligibility, cash waterfall, triggers, representations, servicing, disclosure, retention, and conflicts.
- The crisis lesson is incentive-chain analysis: underwriting, distribution, rating, leverage, short-term funding, and opaque correlation can amplify each other.

### 8. Derivatives and clearing
- Futures, options, and swaps allocate market risks but create counterparty, collateral, liquidity, valuation, and interconnectedness risks.
- For OTC derivatives, analyze documentation and close-out netting, collateral/margin, reporting, trading and clearing mandates, central-counterparty concentration, cross-border rule conflicts, and resolution stays.
- Central clearing mutualizes and manages counterparty risk but concentrates operational and default-management risk; ask who bears tail losses after defaulter resources, margin, and default funds.

### 9. Asset management
- Mutual funds, money-market funds, and hedge funds differ in redemption promise, leverage, liquidity, investor base, disclosure, and regulatory perimeter.
- Assess liquidity mismatch, first-mover advantage, fire-sale externalities, securities lending, derivatives, valuation, custody, and sponsor support expectations.
- Entity designation and activities-based regulation solve different problems; match the regulatory tool to the propagation mechanism.

### 10. Project finance and emerging markets
- Project finance uses a ring-fenced vehicle and contract network to allocate construction, operating, supply, offtake, currency, political, and completion risks.
- Emerging-market analysis must add capital controls, currency mismatch, local institutions, sovereign-bank feedback, creditor diversity, and development-finance involvement.

### 11. Sovereign debt
- Sovereign debt lacks ordinary bankruptcy. Restructuring therefore depends on contract, immunity, asset location, creditor coordination, official-sector policy, and market access.
- Distinguish liquidity from sustainability; domestic from external debt; local-law from foreign-law instruments; official bilateral, multilateral, bank, and bond creditors.
- Collective-action clauses reduce holdout power but do not by themselves resolve inter-creditor, official-sector, or political distribution questions.
- Enforcement analysis requires waiver, commercial-asset exceptions, attachment law, discovery, and the practical scarcity of executable sovereign assets.

### 12. IMF and development finance
- IMF support addresses balance-of-payments and systemic problems through financing plus conditionality; development institutions combine project/policy finance with governance, environmental, and social constraints.
- Analyze mandate, instrument, surveillance/conditionality, governance/voting, seniority expectations, safeguards, burden sharing, and domestic political feasibility.

### 13. Institutional response to instability
- Crisis tools include lender-of-last-resort liquidity, guarantees, recapitalization, asset support, resolution, monetary intervention, capital controls, and international facilities.
- Evaluate each by speed, authority, eligibility, collateral, loss allocation, moral hazard, stigma, cross-border coordination, and exit.
- **Anti-pattern — institution-free prescription**: recommending a tool without checking the body legally empowered and operationally capable of using it.

## Worked Example
A global dealer faces margin calls after a sovereign shock while its money-market affiliates experience redemptions. Build a propagation map across sovereign holdings, repo haircuts, derivatives collateral, fund redemptions, CCP requirements, payment liquidity, and bank capital. Then layer the legal response: central-bank eligibility and collateral, resolution authority, stays and netting, fund liquidity tools, disclosure, and cross-border home/host coordination. Compare alternatives by immediate stabilization, ultimate loss bearer, incentives, and spillovers. The “best” response is the package that preserves critical functions without disguising insolvency as liquidity.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When a rule protects one firm, test whether it shifts risk to affiliates, markets, or infrastructure.
- When cross-border rules diverge, identify the actual legal entity and activity before invoking home/host abstractions.
- When evaluating innovation, compare functional risks and incentives with the nearest conventional product.
- When a crisis tool is proposed, name its legal authority, operational channel, collateral/loss allocation, and exit.
- When using this 2018-era edition, verify every current rule, institutional mandate, threshold, and market practice.

## Key Takeaways
1. Transactions, markets, regulation, and public policy form one causal system.
2. Systemic risk analysis requires a propagation mechanism, not merely a list of large institutions.
3. Infrastructure reforms can transform and concentrate risk as well as reduce it.
4. Sovereign and cross-border crises expose the limits of entity-based national law.
5. Every regulatory recommendation should identify authority, incentives, distribution, and spillovers.
