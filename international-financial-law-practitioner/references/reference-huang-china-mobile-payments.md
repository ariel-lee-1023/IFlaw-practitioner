# “Data Privacy in Mobile Payment” — Robin Hui Huang
**Format**: PDF text extraction of Chapter 6, Fintech Regulation in China | **Sections**: 6 thematic sections | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
Use this chapter as a China-specific case study of mobile-payment platforms, data exploitation, consent, institutional allocation, and enforcement. Do not generalize its historical Chinese rules into universal FinTech principles or assume they remain current.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Mobile-payment market structure
- Identify wallet/payment operator, commercial bank, clearing infrastructure, merchant, customer, platform affiliates, data processors, and regulators.
- Platform scale and ecosystem integration can combine payment data with commerce, social, location, device, and credit information, increasing utility and surveillance/market-power risk.

### 2. Data lifecycle and classification
- Trace collection, authentication, payment instruction, clearing/settlement, analytics, profiling, marketing, sharing, retention, and incident response.
- Distinguish personal, financial, transaction, sensitive, and inferred data under the law applicable at the relevant time; do not rely on source-era labels.

### 3. Consent and disclosure
- Test notice timing, purpose specificity, necessity, bundling, default settings, withdrawal, downstream sharing, and consequences of refusal.
- Formal consent is weakened by information asymmetry, lengthy standard terms, ecosystem lock-in, and unclear secondary uses.

### 4. Platform governance
- Examine internal access, affiliate sharing, profiling, discrimination, cross-selling, security, outsourcing, algorithmic use, and complaint/redress.
- Payment licensing or prudential oversight does not exhaust privacy, cybersecurity, consumer, competition, and data-governance obligations.

### 5. Institutions and enforcement
- Map the source-era roles of central bank/payment supervisors, cybersecurity and sectoral authorities, courts, and private claimants, then update them from current primary Chinese sources.
- Compare administrative supervision/penalties, civil claims, contractual controls, and internal governance. Fragmented mandates can produce gaps or overlapping demands.

### 6. Localization discipline
- Translate any source proposition into: historical rule → current successor rule → responsible authority → regulated entity/activity → enforcement route.
- China-specific concepts, institutional names, thresholds, consent standards, localization/cross-border-transfer rules, and penalties require fresh verification.

## Worked Example
A Chinese mobile-payment platform proposes to use wallet histories and location signals for merchant-credit scoring and share results with an affiliate. Map each data item and entity, original and new purpose, lawful basis/consent, necessity, disclosure, automated-decision effects, access and retention, security, affiliate contract, cross-border transfer, customer challenge, and regulator. Cite only current Chinese primary law for the advice; use Huang to expose the issues and institutional tensions.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When applying this module, label historical analysis and verify current Chinese law.
- When consent is bundled with core payment access, examine whether choice is real and processing necessary.
- When data crosses an affiliate boundary, do not treat common ownership as permission.
- When a platform performs several functions, map each regulator and enforcement route.

## Key Takeaways
1. Mobile-payment data governance is inseparable from platform structure and market power.
2. Consent alone cannot carry the full governance burden.
3. Chinese rules must remain jurisdiction-specific and current-law verified.
