# International Financial Law — Transactions, Regulation, Institutions, and Technology
**Sources**: 15 | **Generated**: 2026-09-03 | **Depth**: study

## How to use
- No args → read this router and pick the source(s).
- “about <issue>” → use the Topic Index to open the relevant reference files.
- “for <source>” → open that source’s distillation directly.
- Most substantial matters need **two or three** files: transaction architecture, the applicable regulatory or institutional layer, and a specialist module for trade, sovereign/IFI, prudential, or data/FinTech risk.

## Operating stance
1. **Map the transaction before stating the rule.** Identify entities, jurisdictions, instruments, assets, currencies, funds/data flows, documents, timeline, and failure scenario.
2. **Separate legal layers.** Contract, capacity/authority, property/perfection/priority, regulation, tax, public international law, procedure, insolvency, and enforcement may be governed by different laws.
3. **Characterize by function, not label.** A “platform,” “guarantee,” “lease,” “participation,” “token,” or “standard” does not answer what it legally does.
4. **Stress the bargain.** Test non-payment, illegality, sanctions, currency controls, operational failure, insolvency, sovereign intervention, and enforcement delay.
5. **Distinguish authority.** Keep binding law, supervisory expectation, international soft standard, market practice, contractual allocation, and analytical framework separate.
6. **Mark the currency seam.** These sources supply durable architecture, not automatically current law. Verify operative propositions, thresholds, institutional powers, and effective dates from primary authority in each relevant jurisdiction.

## Practitioner output discipline
Lead with the executive position and confidence. Then state facts and assumptions, issue map, analysis, prioritized risks, and action plan. For each material current-law proposition identify the jurisdiction, authoritative source still needed, effective date, and precise local-counsel question. Separate legal validity from commercial risk, regulatory permission, tax/accounting, operational feasibility, and enforceability.

## Scope & limits
This library is strong on cross-border loans and bonds, trade finance, security and enforcement, payment/settlement systems, prudential and systemic regulation, internal controls, IFI operations and accountability, sovereign debt, FinTech, financial data, AI governance, and regulatory trade-offs.

It is **source-limited and historically layered**. Wood combines the 1980 first edition with later-edition selected Chapters 7–12, 23–24, and 30; Baber covers available Chapters 1, 2, and 5; Chiu & Wilson covers Chapters 5, 8, and 9; Lee & Darbellay prioritizes Chapters 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, and 11. Huang is a China-specific historical module and must not be generalized.

**When a question falls outside or past the corpus**—current legislation, regulation, institutional mandates, sanctions, market rules, post-source reforms, or a jurisdiction not covered—**say so first, then retrieve primary authority rather than extrapolating**. Mark the seam in the answer: which part rests on this library’s durable architecture and which part rests on current retrieval. Retrieved claims do not inherit the books’ confidence, and historical claims do not become current law through repetition.
