# “Regulatory Agencies and the Inclusion Trilemma” — Chris Brummer
**Format**: pdf | **Pages**: 23 | **Sections**: 6 thematic sections | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
Financial inclusion is not automatically produced by innovation, and access alone is not beneficial inclusion. The **inclusion trilemma** highlights tensions among market integrity, innovation, and financial inclusion—and directs attention to agency mandates, measurement, and distribution.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Inclusion as a regulatory objective
- Distinguish availability, eligibility, take-up, affordability, suitability, quality, continuity, and outcomes. A new account or app can increase nominal access while worsening debt, fraud, exclusion, or data exploitation.
- Identify excluded groups and barriers: price, geography, documentation, disability, language, trust, digital access, data gaps, discrimination, and product mismatch.

### 2. The inclusion trilemma
- **Market integrity** addresses honesty, customer protection, fair dealing, stability, and trust.
- **Innovation** can lower costs and reach new users but introduce opacity, scale, automation, and platform power.
- **Financial inclusion** asks who obtains useful and sustainable access on fair terms.
- A policy can promote one dimension while burdening another; agencies should disclose the trade-off and mitigation rather than invoke innovation as a proxy for inclusion.

### 3. Agency design and tools
- Examine statutory mandate, expertise, data, jurisdiction, enforcement, interagency coordination, and stakeholder participation.
- Tools include tailored authorization, disclosure/conduct duties, pricing or product rules, interoperability, public options, community partnerships, data portability, anti-discrimination enforcement, and experimentation.
- Representation matters: aggregate consultation dominated by incumbents can obscure barriers faced by excluded users.

### 4. Measurement and distribution
- Disaggregate approvals, pricing, errors, complaints, fraud, defaults, closures, service quality, and redress by affected groups while protecting privacy.
- Test long-term outcomes and cross-subsidies, not only adoption. Identify who pays for compliance, fraud, defaults, and infrastructure.
- **Anti-pattern — innovation = inclusion**: counting digital availability without measuring usability, fairness, resilience, or outcomes.

## Worked Example
A mobile lender expands credit using alternative data. Evaluate approval gains for previously excluded applicants alongside price, error, disparate impact, privacy, fraud, repeat borrowing, defaults, complaints, and effective redress. Compare a broad launch, prohibition, and staged regime. Require representative testing and monitoring, explainability appropriate to adverse decisions, correction routes, product safeguards, and exit triggers. State the intended inclusion outcome and the integrity risk being tolerated or mitigated.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When claiming inclusion, define the beneficiary, barrier removed, product quality, and outcome metric.
- When relaxing a rule, identify who bears resulting errors and losses.
- When data show aggregate gains, disaggregate distribution and service quality.
- When an agency lacks mandate or data, coordinate or seek authority rather than hiding the gap.

## Key Takeaways
1. Access is not the same as fair, useful, durable inclusion.
2. Innovation can include and exclude at the same time.
3. Agencies need explicit mandates, representative evidence, and outcome measures.
