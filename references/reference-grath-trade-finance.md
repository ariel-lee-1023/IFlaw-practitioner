# The Handbook of International Trade and Finance — Anders Grath
**Format**: md (third edition) | **Pages**: ~236 | **Sections**: 8 chapters | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
An export sale is unfinished until delivery obligations are performed and usable funds are received. Structure payment, finance, transport documents, insurance, and currency terms as one risk-allocation package. Begin with an individual transaction risk assessment; choose instruments only after identifying what can fail, who controls it, and when bargaining power shifts.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Transaction risk assessment
- Map purchaser/commercial risk, country/political risk, product/performance risk, documentary risk, currency risk, financing/liquidity risk, transport risk, and bank risk.
- Rate each by probability, loss severity, duration, evidence available, control/mitigation, and residual exposure. Reassess when country, buyer, product, route, tenor, or contract changes.
- **Anti-pattern — precedent terms**: reusing familiar payment terms after circumstances change or leaving terms undefined until after shipment.

### 2. Payment methods as a risk ladder
- **Advance payment** favors seller cash and credit risk but burdens buyer performance risk.
- **Open account** favors buyer and shifts collection, country, and financing risk to seller.
- **Bank transfer/remittance** is a payment channel, not independent security; specify amount, currency, value date, charges, and discharge.
- **Documentary collection** routes documents through banks under collection instructions; banks handle documents but do not promise payment. Distinguish documents against payment (D/P) from documents against acceptance (D/A).
- **Documentary credit / letter of credit** substitutes a bank’s documentary undertaking for buyer credit, subject to exact terms and applicable rules. Independence and strict/documentary compliance mean banks examine documents, not goods.

### 3. Documentary-credit design
- Align commercial contract, credit application, credit text, transport document, invoice, insurance, inspection/certificates, Incoterms allocation, shipment dates, presentation period, and expiry/place.
- Decide issuing, advising, confirming, nominated, and reimbursing bank roles. Confirmation can mitigate issuing-bank and country risk but adds cost and document conditions.
- Before shipment, conduct a **documentability review**: can the seller produce every required document, with consistent data, within time and through the intended route?
- **Anti-pattern — soft clauses**: conditions dependent on buyer-controlled documents or facts not evidenced by a stipulated document.

### 4. Bonds, guarantees, and standby credits
- Identify underlying performance risk and beneficiary demand standard before choosing bid, advance-payment, performance, retention, payment, customs, or warranty security.
- A **demand guarantee** or standby letter of credit is ordinarily documentary and independent; a surety/conditional guarantee may require proof of underlying default. Governing rules and wording control.
- Negotiate amount/reduction, expiry, presentation place/method, required statement/documents, extend-or-pay language, transfer, governing rules, sanctions, and counter-guarantee chain.
- Protect against unfair calling through precise triggers and project controls, not assumptions that the bank will investigate the merits.

### 5. Currency risk management
- Identify transaction exposure from quotation through payment, including uncertain amount/timing and financing currency. Distinguish economic/competitive and translation exposure.
- Allocate risk through pricing currency, adjustment clauses, matching receipts/payments, leading/lagging, netting, or financial hedges such as forwards and options.
- Hedge the underlying exposure and tenor; do not turn risk reduction into an unapproved speculative position.

### 6. Export-credit insurance
- Separate commercial and political risks, insured percentage, waiting period, deductible, premium, credit limits, exclusions, notification, loss-minimization duties, claims evidence, and recoveries/subrogation.
- Private insurers and export-credit agencies differ in appetite, policy purpose, country capacity, tenor, and tied-content rules. Insurance is not a substitute for buyer diligence or compliant documentation.

### 7. Supplier and buyer credit
- **Supplier credit**: seller permits deferred payment and funds/insures the receivable; price, interest, title/security, acceptance, and assignment matter.
- **Buyer credit**: a bank or institution finances the foreign buyer, ordinarily paying the exporter against compliant milestones/documents; separates exporter cash receipt from buyer repayment tenor.
- Pre-shipment finance funds production before an export receivable exists; link drawdown, borrowing base, inventory, purchase order, performance milestones, and take-out source.

### 8. Factoring, forfaiting, leasing, and structured trade finance
- Factoring combines receivables purchase/finance with administration, collection, and sometimes credit protection; determine recourse, notification, disputes/dilution, eligibility, and cross-border assignment effectiveness.
- Forfaiting is typically non-recourse purchase of medium-term export receivables evidenced or supported by negotiable instruments/guarantees; validate obligor, aval/guarantee, transfer, sanctions, and discount economics.
- International leasing separates asset use from ownership; test title, tax/accounting, maintenance, insurance, repossession, import, registration, and residual value.
- Structured trade finance relies on identifiable trade flows, controlled goods/documents/accounts, and self-liquidating proceeds. Examine commodity, warehouse, inspection, fraud, commingling, price, logistics, and enforcement risks.

### 9. Terms-of-payment architecture
- Draft a complete operational sequence: contract → conditions → shipment/performance → documents → presentation/acceptance → finance → payment → claims and dispute.
- State payment method, currency, amount, payer/payee accounts, timing trigger, documents, bank costs, interest, partial shipment/payment, set-off, taxes, and consequences of delay or discrepancy.
- Check shipping documents against title/control assumptions; documentary possession may not equal title, security, or control under local law.

## Worked Example
An exporter sells machinery to a higher-risk jurisdiction with six months’ manufacture and two years’ buyer repayment. Allocate advance payment to cover mobilization, use a confirmed documentary credit for shipment value, require a demand guarantee for local bank support only if country and counter-guarantee risk are acceptable, insure political/commercial repayment risk, and arrange buyer credit so the exporter receives cash against documents. Hedge the firm purchase and deferred-payment currency exposures separately. Before signing, reconcile contract milestones, Incoterms, credit documents, inspection certificates, shipment route, insurance, guarantee expiry, and finance draw conditions in one timeline.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When seller leverage falls after shipment, resolve ambiguity and security before shipment.
- When choosing a payment method, compare residual buyer, bank, country, documentary, and performance risk—not instrument labels.
- When a credit demands a document the seller cannot independently obtain, amend it before performance.
- When insuring or financing receivables, preserve notice, reporting, mitigation, and assignment requirements from day one.
- When structuring trade flows, verify physical control and fraud resilience independently of paper documentation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Terms of payment are a negotiated risk package, not boilerplate.
2. Documentary instruments protect only against risks their documents and undertakings actually cover.
3. Finance, insurance, currency, logistics, and contract performance must share one timeline.
4. Country-neutral principles require current local-law and sanctions checks.
