# Law and Practice of International Finance — Philip R. Wood
**Format**: pdf (OCR; 1980 first edition plus later-edition selected chapters) | **Pages**: ~554 | **Sections**: 21 logical chapters | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
Treat every cross-border financing as a stack of legal systems, documents, parties, assets, payment channels, and insolvency outcomes. The practitioner’s task is not to find one governing law, but to allocate each issue to the law that actually controls it, then make the documents, conditions precedent, opinions, perfection steps, and enforcement plan cohere. Wood’s recurring test is practical: what happens to the creditor’s bargain when authority fails, payment stops, an asset moves, a sovereign intervenes, or insolvency overrides the contract?

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Conflict-of-laws matrix
- **Two-stage conflicts analysis** — first identify the legal system governing the issue; only then determine that law’s content. Do not let a governing-law clause answer capacity, corporate authority, proprietary effect, perfection, priority, insolvency, or procedure when another connecting factor controls.
- Build an issue matrix covering: contract formation and interpretation; status and capacity; authority; situs and proprietary effect; perfection and priority; payment currency and place; forum and procedure; insolvency; and recognition/enforcement.
- **Anti-pattern — single-law thinking**: saying “English law governs” without identifying foreign mandatory rules, debtor incorporation law, asset situs law, or forum law.

### 2. Governing law, forum, and enforcement
- Choose governing law for predictability and market practice, but separately test whether the chosen forum will take jurisdiction, whether interim relief is available, and where a judgment or award must be enforced.
- Map jurisdiction clauses as exclusive/non-exclusive and asymmetric where applicable; check service, parallel proceedings, forum non conveniens, arbitration scope, and judgment/arbitral-award recognition.
- Treat procedural remedies, limitation, evidence, and execution as forum questions unless local law characterizes them otherwise.

### 3. Sovereign risk and exchange controls
- Split sovereign risk into capacity/authority, sovereign immunity from suit, immunity from execution, act-of-state or comparable doctrines, political intervention, currency controls, and practical asset location.
- Obtain express waivers broad enough to cover proceedings, relief, attachment, execution, and relevant categories of assets, but verify local non-waivable immunities and whether assets are commercial or sovereign/public.
- Exchange-control analysis asks whether payment, borrowing, security, conversion, or remittance needs authorization; whether non-compliance affects validity or only performance; and whether the forum recognizes the foreign control.

### 4. Syndicated bank loans
- **Term loan** — committed finance for a fixed term, ordinarily cancellable or repayable early only on agreed events; contrast a callable overdraft or line of credit. Revolving, multicurrency, standby, and tranche structures trade certainty against operational complexity.
- Separate roles: arranger structures and syndicates; agent administers communications and payments; security trustee/agent holds or administers collateral; each lender usually has a several commitment.
- Check conditions precedent, utilization mechanics, interest and benchmark provisions, increased costs, tax gross-up, illegality, market disruption, assignments/transfers, confidentiality, and lender voting.
- Payment to the agent normally discharges the borrower because the agent acts for the lenders; verify the drafting, especially around agent failure and replacement.

### 5. Covenants as the lender’s “vote”
- Debt lacks equity voting rights; covenants provide a muted governance voice by making specified deterioration or conduct actionable through default.
- Organize covenants as information, affirmative, negative, and financial. Calibrate baskets, thresholds, permitted transactions, cure rights, materiality, and group coverage to the credit thesis.
- **Negative pledge** protects against structural or contractual subordination but is not a security interest. **Pari passu** ordinarily addresses equal ranking of unsecured obligations; it should not be drafted as a disguised negative pledge.
- **Anti-pattern — covenant accumulation**: importing precedent clauses without connecting each restriction to a monitored risk and a usable consequence.

### 6. Events of default, acceleration, and collective action
- Distinguish contract breaches (non-payment, covenant breach, misrepresentation) from external deterioration (cross-default, insolvency, unlawfulness, repudiation, material adverse change).
- Test each event for trigger, knowledge, materiality, grace/cure, evidence, and consequences. Default typically supports cancellation, acceleration, enforcement, and sometimes indemnity or heightened interest.
- Insolvency law may stay or invalidate cancellation, acceleration, set-off, security enforcement, and dispositions regardless of governing law.
- Majority-lender provisions enable collective waivers and amendments, but reserved matters protect the economic core. Address disenfranchisement, snooze-you-lose/yank-the-bank mechanisms where appropriate, and good-faith constraints on majority power.

### 7. Loan transfer and participation
- Compare assignment of rights, transfer/novation of rights and obligations, funded or risk participation, and sub-participation. Each changes privity, confidentiality, set-off exposure, security benefit, licensing, tax, and regulatory treatment differently.
- A participation can leave the participant exposed to the grantor rather than directly to the borrower. For a true transfer, verify consent, notice, registration, minimum hold, and accession mechanics.
- **Pro rata sharing** is a syndicate-equality device: a lender receiving a disproportionate recovery shares the excess, subject to negotiated exclusions and restoration if the recovery is later reversed.

### 8. International bonds
- Bonds are transferable debt securities sold to investors, commonly through managers/underwriters, with a trustee or fiscal agent structure and clearing-system holdings rather than paper delivery.
- Compare bonds with syndicated loans on lender identity, disclosure, transferability, amendment thresholds, trustee action, enforcement, and public-offering regulation.
- For equity-linked or convertible bonds, trace conversion price, adjustment/anti-dilution, corporate authority, share availability, and change-of-control consequences.
- Bondholder meetings and collective-action terms must reconcile majority restructuring with protected payment rights and applicable mandatory law.

### 9. Prospectus regulation and market abuse
- Financial assets are sold through description; disclosure law therefore substitutes verified information for physical inspection. Identify whether an offer is public/private, the regulator and venue, required content, approval, exemptions, selling restrictions, and liability recipients.
- Diligence must address material misstatement and omission, expert reports, incorporation by reference, responsibility statements, and cross-border distribution.
- Keep three fraud layers distinct: general fraud/misrepresentation, market manipulation, and insider dealing. Determine civil, administrative, and criminal exposure separately.

### 10. Guarantees and security
- Characterize a promise as guarantee, indemnity, demand obligation, keepwell, comfort, or security; labels do not control. A guarantee is commonly secondary and vulnerable to defenses or discharge when the principal obligation changes; an indemnity or demand obligation may be primary.
- For security, identify grantor capacity, asset description, attachment/creation, perfection/publicity, priority, control or possession, proceeds, enforcement route, and insolvency avoidance. Repeat by asset and jurisdiction.
- A security package is only as strong as its weakest local-law assumption: registrations lapse, assets migrate, receivables require notice, and floating security may rank behind statutory priorities.

### 11. Project and asset finance
- Project finance depends on cash-flow sufficiency and risk allocation more than balance-sheet recourse. Map construction, completion, technology, supply, offtake, operating, political, currency, environmental, and force-majeure risks to the party best able to control or insure them.
- Require bankability across the contract suite: aligned definitions, term, termination, liquidated damages, step-in/direct agreements, assignments, insurance, reserve accounts, and waterfall.
- In ship or aircraft finance, add title/registration, mortgage law, flag or registry, insurance assignment, total-loss treatment, requisition, arrest, maintenance, location, and international recognition.

### 12. Tax, legal opinions, and closing discipline
- Tax analysis covers withholding, gross-up, tax indemnity, lender qualification, treaty access, transfer consequences, stamp/documentary taxes, and permanent-establishment risk. Treat it as time-sensitive.
- Legal opinions allocate legal-verification work; they do not insure facts or commercial success. Define addressees, transaction documents, assumptions, qualifications, capacity/authority, enforceability, choice of law, jurisdiction, and security opinions precisely.
- Closing is a dependency graph: constitutional approvals, authorizations, KYC, documents, perfection, funds flow, opinions, and post-closing filings must be owned, sequenced, and evidenced.

### 13. Payment and securities-settlement systems
- Large-value payment uses **bank money**: a sequence of obligations, debits, and credits through originator, banks, settlement banks, central bank, and beneficiary—not physical cash or a simple assignment.
- Identify when payment becomes final, revocable, and discharged; intermediary-bank risk; cut-off and value date; erroneous or fraudulent instruction allocation; and applicable system rules.
- Settlement systems concentrate systemic risk. Analyze netting, collateral, delivery-versus-payment, settlement finality, participant default, operational resilience, and insolvency safe harbors under current law.

## Worked Example
A lender syndicate finances a foreign project company under English-law documents, secured over local shares, accounts, receivables, and plant. Build four linked schedules: (1) transaction parties and cash flows; (2) issue-by-issue governing laws; (3) risk allocation across construction, offtake, currency, political action, and operations; and (4) enforcement waterfall. Confirm borrower authority under incorporation law, security creation/perfection under each asset situs or registry law, exchange-control approvals, sovereign waivers in the offtake/direct agreement, agent and voting mechanics, payment finality, and local insolvency overrides. Make each condition precedent answer a named failure mode. The closing opinion should state only the legal conclusions actually required and disclose assumptions that the deal team must independently verify.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When a conclusion depends on “the governing law,” split it into contract, status, property, insolvency, and procedure before relying on it.
- When a covenant has no monitored credit rationale or usable remedy, delete or redesign it.
- When changing an underlying obligation, re-check guarantee preservation, security scope, priority, and opinion coverage.
- When enforcement matters, analyze asset location and immunity from execution, not merely consent to jurisdiction.
- When using a payment or clearing system, verify current finality and insolvency rules; historical descriptions are orientation only.
- When a source states a statutory or regulatory rule, confirm the rule and commencement date in every relevant jurisdiction before advising or drafting.

## Key Takeaways
1. Cross-border finance is an issue-by-issue conflicts exercise, not a single governing-law choice.
2. Drafting should convert the credit thesis into conditions, covenants, defaults, voting, and enforcement mechanics.
3. Security, guarantees, netting, and settlement protections must survive local insolvency law.
4. Opinions and closing checklists allocate verification; they do not cure missing facts or filings.
5. Wood’s historical rules require current-law verification, while his transaction decomposition remains durable.
